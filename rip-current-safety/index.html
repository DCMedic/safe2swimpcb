Know before you go

Rip current safety.

Rip currents are narrow, fast-moving channels of water flowing away from shore. They can carry even strong swimmers offshore. The safest strategy is prevention: check conditions, swim near lifeguards and know what to do before entering the water.

What a rip current does

A rip current moves water away from shore through the surf zone. It can form near breaks in sandbars and alongside piers, jetties and other structures. A rip current does not pull a swimmer underwater; the danger comes from being carried away from shore, becoming exhausted and trying to fight the current head-on.

If you are caught in a rip current

Stay calm and conserve energy. Do not try to overpower the current by swimming directly back toward shore.

Move out of the current. Swim in a direction following the shoreline, or toward breaking waves, and then angle back toward the beach once you are free of the seaward flow.

If you cannot escape, float or tread water. Keep your airway clear, conserve energy, and wave and yell for help.

If someone else is in trouble

Get a lifeguard immediately. If no lifeguard is present, call 911. Direct the person to move parallel to the shoreline if they can hear you and throw them something that floats. Do not enter the water without flotation; rescuers can become victims themselves.

Before you enter the Gulf

Check the local forecastUse the National Weather Service beach forecast and local rip-current outlook before leaving for the beach.
Read the posted flagFlorida beach flags communicate overall surf hazards, but flags are not a dedicated rip-current forecast.
Swim near lifeguardsLifeguards know the local sandbars, currents, changing surf and current hazard areas.
Stay away from structuresRip currents commonly develop near piers and jetties. NOAA advises keeping clear of these areas when swimming.

Sunny weather can still produce dangerous currents

Blue skies and warm water do not guarantee safe swimming conditions. Rip currents can occur on otherwise pleasant beach days, and swell from distant storms or tropical systems can increase the hazard even when the storm itself is far away.
